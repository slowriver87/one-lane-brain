One Lane Brain

Two quick decisions, a split second apart. See how long the second one waits.

About 2 to 3 minutes

Left thumb: tap the color. Right thumb: tap the arrow. Left first.

How to play

The left box turns blue or orange. Tap the matching color with your left thumb. The right box shows an arrow, up or down. Tap the matching arrow with your right thumb.

Part 1 shows the right box alone, to get your normal speed. In part 2 both boxes appear, the right one a moment after the left. Answer the left box first, then the right, each as fast as you can.

On a computer: A and S for the colors, K and L for the arrows. To stop partway, hold the Quit button for one second, or press Esc twice.

The science behind this game

What it measures

This game measures how long a second quick decision has to wait when it comes right after a first one. Researchers call this the psychological refractory period. Your score is the extra time your right-thumb answer takes when the right box appears 50 ms after the left box, compared with when it appears a full second later.

How this compares to the lab. Lab studies use the same basic design: two choice tasks, a variable gap between them, and the slowdown of the second response at short gaps. Labs usually average many more trials per gap. This game uses 6 per gap and drops the fastest and slowest correct trial at each gap, so one slip doesn't dominate.

What the research says

Choosing is the bottleneck. A major review of two-task studies concluded there is a stubborn bottleneck in the process of choosing actions [1]. Seeing both boxes can happen at once. Picking the response can't.

The one-lane prediction. If choices truly go one at a time, then for every 100 ms sooner the second box appears, the second answer should wait about 100 ms longer, until the gap is long enough that the first choice is already done. That's the slope this game reports. This is our plain-English version of what the bottleneck model implies.

Practice shrinks it, but it stays. In one study, the wait started at 353 ms and shrank to about 40 ms over many practice sessions [3]. A follow-up found the bottleneck still existed even after extensive practice. The wait mostly shrank because people got faster at the first task [2].

It shows up behind the wheel. In a driving simulator, people's braking got slower the closer a quick choice task came before the moment they needed to brake [4].

Adult norms

No published adult norm exists for this game. The size of the wait depends heavily on which two tasks are used. For reference only: the study above measured 353 ms before practice, using a tone task answered by voice plus a visual task answered by hand [3]. That's a different task pair, so it isn't a fair comparison for this game.

What's still debated

Some researchers argue the evidence doesn't require a strict one-at-a-time gate. They say most findings can also be explained if the two tasks share a limited pool of mental capacity [5]. Both views predict that the second answer slows down at short gaps. They disagree on whether any real overlap happens.

Limits of this game

One round is noisy. In our simulation of 3,000 rounds by players with a built-in bottleneck, one round's wait landed within about ±140 ms of the true value 95% of the time. Pooling 10 rounds narrows that to about ±60 ms. The scoring showed no lean high or low.

Phones add noise. Screen refresh and touch timing add a few hundredths of a second of jitter to every tap.

Practice changes the score. As the research above shows, the wait shrinks with practice. So your score reflects how practiced you are at this game, not a fixed trait.

The simulation is a model. Its speeds and noise levels are our assumptions. Your own consistency, shown once you settle, is the better guide for you.
