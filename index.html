Why the second answer waits

Seeing is parallel. Deciding is not. Your eyes take in both boxes at once. But choosing which button to press goes through one channel. While it is busy with the left box, the right box's decision sits in line.

What the chart shows. When the right box appears long after the left, there is no line, and you answer at your normal speed. When it appears almost at once, you have to wait for the left decision to finish first. So the shorter the gap, the slower the right answer.

The slope is the fingerprint. If decisions truly go one at a time, then every 100 ms sooner the right box appears adds about 100 ms of waiting. A slope near 100 ms per 100 ms means pure single-file processing. A smaller slope means some overlap.

Your left answer barely changes. The left box is always first in line, so its speed stays about the same at every gap. Only the second decision pays.

What this can't tell you. 30 trials is a small sample, and a phone's screen and touch timing add a few hundredths of a second of noise. Lab studies use hundreds of trials and special equipment. The history table smooths things out over several runs. The pattern is what matters more than any single number.

The science

  • First noticed nearly a century ago. Telford found that a second quick response was slowed when it came right after a first one. He called the delay a refractory period, borrowing the term from nerve cells. Telford, 1931
  • The single-channel idea. Welford proposed that the brain has one decision channel that handles one choice at a time. The second choice must wait until the first is done. Welford, 1952
  • Pinned down with this exact setup. Pashler reviewed decades of two-task experiments like this game. Perception and pressing the button can overlap, but choosing the response cannot. The slope of minus one at short gaps became the signature. Pashler, 1994
  • Found in the brain. Brain scans during this kind of task showed one area, in the back of the side of the prefrontal cortex, that handled both decisions one after the other. It acted like the bottleneck itself. Dux and colleagues, 2006
  • It happens even when you're told which to do first. Knowing the order and having practice doesn't remove it. Heavy practice can shrink it, but careful studies usually find a small delay remains. Ruthruff and colleagues, 2003
  • It shows up on the road. In a driving simulator, drivers who had to make a quick choice just before the car ahead braked hit their own brakes later. The shorter the gap, the bigger the delay. Same curve as this game. Levy, Pashler and Boer, 2006

What's still debated. Some researchers think it's not a strict one-at-a-time gate but a shared pool of capacity, where the first task simply grabs most of it. Both views predict the same basic curve. They differ on whether any overlap is possible. Evidence exists for both, and the answer may depend on the task.

Based on the psychological refractory period: Telford 1931, Welford 1952, Pashler 1994.
